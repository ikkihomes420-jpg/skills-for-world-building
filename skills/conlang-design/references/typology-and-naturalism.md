# Typology, Naturalism, and Responsible Inspiration

Use this reference when selecting structural features, aiming for naturalistic plausibility, or checking whether a design has become a disconnected feature list. Cross-linguistic databases such as WALS are useful for discovering the range and distribution of structural strategies, not for declaring that a frequent pattern is inherently superior or that a rare pattern requires justification.[1]

## Use Typology as a Constraint Network

Choose a small number of high-leverage decisions, then inspect the systems they pressure. State a default analysis and document exceptions.

| Starting decision | Inspect next | Typical failure if ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Head-final clauses | Adpositions, genitives, adjective order, auxiliaries, complementizers, relative clauses | A random mix of correlations with no social or historical account. |
| Rich case system | Noun phrase marking, agreement, adposition competition, word order, pronouns | Cases that have no distribution or overlap without semantic motivation. |
| Ergative or split alignment | Pronouns, case, agreement, coordination, relativization, TAM conditions | A label applied to one paradigm while syntax behaves without explanation. |
| Heavy agglutination | Morpheme order, boundary phonology, scope, productivity, word length | Affix chains whose meanings and ordering cannot be parsed. |
| Tone or complex stress | Morphology, compounds, loans, intonation, orthography | A tone list with no stated domain or interaction. |
| Extensive honorification | Pronouns, address terms, verbs, discourse particles, setting | A single honorific word with no social distribution. |
| Strong contact setting | Loan strata, phonology, semantics, register, orthography | “Borrowed flavor” without chronology or adaptation rules. |

## Naturalism Is Causal, Not Ornamental

Naturalistic design depends on patterns being explainable through language use, historical change, contact, acquisition, frequency, analogy, social variation, or genre. It does **not** require copying a real language or adding arbitrary difficulty.

| Weak move | Stronger move |
|---|---|
| Add random irregular verbs. | Derive a bounded irregular class from sound change, high frequency, suppletion, or analogy. |
| Mix rare features to feel original. | Define a communicative/community use case and build compatible systems around it. |
| Copy a real language's words or grammar. | Identify an abstract structural inspiration, alter the system through independent constraints, and document the distinction. |
| Declare a pattern “universal.” | State it as a project generalization and investigate cross-linguistic alternatives. |
| Use an exoticized cultural stereotype. | Define concrete speaker practices, institutions, material culture, and contact conditions. |

## Conduct a Typology Pass

1. State the feature question precisely, such as “How is a possessive relation encoded in ordinary noun phrases?” rather than “Should the language have genitive?”
2. Consult a specific source or feature chapter when real-language variation matters. WALS organizes phonological, grammatical, and lexical features and advises citing the specific chapter used for a claim.[1]
3. Compare strategies, their conditioning environments, and their structural consequences rather than copying a surface marker.
4. Choose a project-specific design and record its functional/historical rationale.
5. Test it against the rest of the grammar in contrasting sentences and connected text.
6. Record any exception as lexical, social, structural, or historical—not as an unexplained anomaly.

## Avoid Misleading Claims

Do not infer real-world frequency from a single data source or a small sample. Do not equate typological “rarity” with implausibility. Do not make cultural, genetic, or cognitive claims about imagined speakers from a grammatical feature. Treat maps and descriptive grammars as evidence to investigate, not an authority that can replace analysis.
