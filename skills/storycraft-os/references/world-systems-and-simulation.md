# World Architecture and Simulation Protocol

Read this reference when creating or extending a setting, simulating a world turn, designing an alternate history, resolving faction activity, building a campaign state, or checking whether a world reacts credibly. Use it as a causal modeling workflow, not an excuse to force every project into realism.

## 1. Start With a World Brief

Define what the world is for, its scale, intended uses, reality model, expected degree of simulation, and a completion gate. A local campaign city needs different records from a planetary alternate history. Write a design promise and list constraints before generating factions, map details, or historical epochs.

| Scale | Minimum viable model | Do not add by default |
|---|---|---|
| Local | Place, inhabitants, material life, local power, one external connection, active pressure. | Global chronology and exhaustive pantheons. |
| Regional | Geography, trade/communication, multiple polities/cultures, historical tension, linked systems. | Planetary climate model without a use case. |
| Planetary | Biomes, population distribution, power blocs, major information/transport systems, macro history. | Full detail for every state or language. |
| Interstellar/multiversal | Travel/communication constraints, governance scale, resource logistics, ontological rules, divergence control. | Handwaved distances and power capacities. |

## 2. Build From Constraints to Consequences

For every declared fact, ask what it enables, constrains, costs, and makes likely. Begin with high-leverage conditions: geography/ecology, material capacity, demography, transport, information technology, resource concentration, metaphysics, and power legitimacy.

```text
condition → capability/constraint → incentive → actor response → consequence → changed condition
```

Use this chain to find second-order effects. A magical water source may change settlement, ritual authority, military strategy, trade, disease, agricultural specialization, class power, and social metaphor. Stop when additional effects no longer influence the user’s purpose.

## 3. Model Systems as Interfaces

| System | Core variables | Cross-system links to test |
|---|---|---|
| Geography/ecology | Terrain, climate, water, disease, seasons, carrying capacity. | Food, travel, defense, settlement, ritual. |
| Material economy | Production, labor, scarcity, trade, debt, tax, storage. | Class, war capacity, migration, institution incentives. |
| Polity/power | Legitimacy, coercion, administration, succession, oversight. | Factions, law, corruption/integrity, public narrative. |
| Culture/religion | Kinship, values, taboo, time, death, status, metaphors. | Law, gender, diplomacy, art, everyday behavior. |
| Information | Literacy, archives, rumor, press, surveillance, magic/tech channels. | Reputation, secrets, decision delay, misinformation. |
| Technology/magic | Preconditions, costs, access, reliability, bottlenecks, adoption. | Inequality, warfare, work, ecological disruption, governance. |

Mark unknown interfaces as **open decisions**, not implicit assumptions. An interface is more valuable than a glossary entry because it constrains future generation.

## 4. Create Actors That Can Disagree

Every named faction, institution, and major character needs a goal, resources, capability, constraint, belief, information state, and relationship map. Give actors internal divisions and a distinction between public position and private agenda when useful. Do not let every actor agree because an idea is rational; do not make opposition automatically malicious or correct.

For a simulation turn, update actors from their own goals and knowledge before responding to the protagonist/player. An absent actor has not been frozen; it has been pursuing or failing at its priorities off-screen.

## 5. Simulate History and Counterfactual Change

Freeze a source state before deriving a new one. For alternate history, define the divergence point, the conditions immediately changed, and the pathways that remain unchanged unless affected. Track consequences at multiple scales.

| Scale | Typical horizon | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| Personal | Days to years | Who changes behavior, trust, health, or livelihood? |
| Relational | Weeks to years | What alliances, families, rivalries, or obligations change? |
| Institutional | Years to decades | What policy, organization, revenue, doctrine, or norm changes? |
| Structural | Decades | What trade, technology, demography, or power balance shifts? |
| Civilizational | Generations | What language, memory, geography of power, or worldview transforms? |

A real historical event is neither forced nor ignored. Recalculate how entities with relevant links, information channels, and interests would notice, reinterpret, or be affected by it. Record uncertainty rather than narrating hindsight as contemporary knowledge.

## 6. Resolve an Interactive Turn

For roleplay, games, and simulations, follow this order:

1. Read the current session state, active entities, knowledge items, open threads, and consequences.
2. Advance off-screen actors and systems according to their goals, capacities, and constraints.
3. Apply the user/player’s action; resolve only what existing rules and information support.
4. Update facts, beliefs, relationships, resources, timeline, and consequence chains.
5. Identify whether a high-impact player choice is imminent. If it is, render the pressure and stop; do not make the choice for the player.
6. Produce the requested output mode: immersive prose, world-state update, decision council, lore entry, map brief, or audit.
7. Update the active state ledger and flag drift or unresolved contradiction.

## 7. Use Calibrated World Health Checks

Monitor recurring failure patterns across a span of work: frictionless institutions, protagonist/player immunity, static off-screen world, repeated social perspective, consequence chains that never surface, lore that ignores material constraints, knowledge that teleports, or one tonal/craft correction used so often that it becomes a tic.

Correct by returning to established state, surfacing a relevant existing pressure, changing vantage rather than inventing arbitrary crisis, and recalibrating intensity. A healthy world contains variation: cooperation and conflict, integrity and self-interest, beauty and cost, discovery and uncertainty.
